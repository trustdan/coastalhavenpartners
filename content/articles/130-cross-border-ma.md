# Cross-Border M&A: How International Deals Differ and What Makes Them Work

In 2016, Bayer announced its acquisition of Monsanto for $66 billion. A German company buying an American agricultural giant.

The deal took over two years to close. Regulatory approvals were required in 30 countries. The political scrutiny was intense. The integration challenges were immense. And the deal ultimately destroyed significant shareholder value.

Cross-border M&A is hard. It's more complex than domestic deals. It takes longer. It costs more. And it fails more often.

Yet it remains a third of all M&A activity. Companies pursue cross-border deals for strategic reasons that justify the complexity. The key is understanding what makes these deals different—and how to manage the additional risks.

---

## Why Cross-Border M&A Happens

### The Strategic Rationale

Companies pursue cross-border acquisitions for several reasons:

**Market access:**
Acquire a platform to enter new geographic markets. Buying an established local player is often faster than organic expansion.

**Technology and capabilities:**
Acquire technology, IP, or expertise that exists abroad but not domestically.

**Talent:**
Access engineering, research, or other talent pools in specific regions.

**Scale and consolidation:**
Global industries consolidate across borders. Scale economics require international combination.

**Resource access:**
Natural resources, manufacturing capacity, or supply chain capabilities in specific locations.

**Diversification:**
Geographic and economic diversification reduces risk exposure.

### The Data

Cross-border M&A represents significant activity:

| Metric | Typical Range |
|--------|---------------|
| Share of global M&A | 30-40% |
| Average deal size | Higher than domestic |
| Time to close | 50%+ longer than domestic |
| Regulatory complexity | Significantly higher |
| Integration failure rate | Higher than domestic |

The deals are bigger and more strategic—but also more difficult.

## What Makes Cross-Border Different

### Regulatory Complexity

Domestic deals typically need approval from one competition authority. Cross-border deals may need approval from many.

**Multi-jurisdictional review:**
A deal involving a U.S. acquirer and European target may require:
- U.S. (DOJ/FTC, CFIUS)
- EU Commission
- UK CMA
- German Bundeskartellamt
- Plus any other country where either party has significant operations

**Different standards:**
Each jurisdiction applies different tests. A deal cleared in the U.S. may face challenges in Europe or vice versa.

**Timing coordination:**
Filing requirements and review timelines differ. Coordinating across jurisdictions is complex.

**CFIUS and national security:**
Foreign acquisitions of U.S. companies face Committee on Foreign Investment review. Expanding to cover more transactions and scrutinizing more closely.

**Other foreign investment regimes:**
Europe, UK, Australia, and others have strengthened foreign investment screening. National security considerations increasingly relevant.

### Legal and Structural Differences

**Corporate law varies:**
Deal structures, board processes, and shareholder rights differ by jurisdiction.

**Employment law varies:**
European works councils, consultation requirements, and termination rules differ dramatically from U.S. norms.

**Contract law varies:**
Material adverse change clauses, representations and warranties, and indemnification work differently.

**Tax complexity:**
Cross-border transactions create complex tax planning challenges and opportunities.

### Cultural Differences

**Negotiation styles:**
American directness vs. Asian relationship-building vs. European formality. Miscommunication is common.

**Decision-making processes:**
Consensus cultures vs. hierarchical cultures. Speed varies significantly.

**Integration approaches:**
Cultural integration is the top failure factor in cross-border M&A.

## The Cross-Border Deal Process

### Sourcing and Origination

**Advisor role is crucial:**
Local advisors with on-the-ground relationships and knowledge are essential.

**Language and access:**
English is common in finance, but local language may be necessary for management access.

**Relationship building:**
Cross-border deals often require longer relationship development before transaction discussions.

### Due Diligence

**Expanded scope:**
Cross-border due diligence must cover jurisdiction-specific issues.

| Area | Cross-Border Additions |
|------|----------------------|
| Financial | Local GAAP vs. IFRS differences, currency, transfer pricing |
| Legal | Local law compliance, employment, real estate |
| Tax | International tax structures, withholding, transfer pricing |
| Regulatory | Multi-jurisdiction requirements, foreign investment |
| HR | Local labor law, works councils, benefit obligations |
| Cultural | Management assessment, cultural fit evaluation |

**Local expertise:**
Local lawyers, accountants, and advisors are essential. Not optional.

**Time and cost:**
Due diligence takes longer and costs more. Budget accordingly.

### Valuation

**Currency considerations:**
Which currency for valuation? How to handle FX exposure?

**Country risk adjustments:**
Discount rates may need country-specific adjustments.

**Comparable selection:**
Domestic vs. international comparables. Different multiples in different markets.

**Synergy realism:**
Cross-border synergies are harder to achieve. Apply appropriate haircuts.

### Structuring

**Legal structure:**
Which entity acquires? Holding company considerations. Repatriation of cash.

**Tax efficiency:**
International tax planning is complex and heavily scrutinized.

**Financing:**
Where is debt raised? Currency matching. Local banking relationships.

**Consideration:**
Cash, stock, or mixed. FX hedging for foreign currency components.

### Regulatory Approval

**Multi-track process:**
Coordinate filings across jurisdictions with different timelines.

**Strategy:**
Decide which jurisdictions are likely problematic. May need remedies (divestitures) to clear.

**Timeline risk:**
Regulatory delays are common. Can extend deals by months or years.

**Break fee and termination:**
Regulatory failure risk must be allocated in deal documentation.

### Integration

**The hardest part:**
Integration is where cross-border deals most often fail.

**Cultural integration:**
Different management styles, communication patterns, and expectations.

**Operating model:**
How much integration vs. autonomy? What's the target operating model?

**Talent retention:**
Key people in acquired company must be retained through transition.

**Communication:**
Language barriers and communication styles create friction.

## Regional Considerations

### United States Targets

**Legal framework:**
Delaware corporate law governs most deals. Hostile deals possible.

**Regulatory:**
- DOJ/FTC antitrust review
- CFIUS for foreign acquirers (increasingly strict)
- SEC requirements for public targets
- Industry-specific (banking, insurance, defense, etc.)

**Deal dynamics:**
- Shareholder approval required
- Break fees typically 3-4% for public deals
- Fiduciary out provisions standard
- Go-shop provisions possible

### European Targets

**Legal framework:**
Varies by country. EU-level rules (Takeover Directive) provide some harmonization.

**Regulatory:**
- EU Commission for large deals (turnover thresholds)
- National competition authorities for smaller deals
- Growing foreign investment screening (especially post-COVID)
- Works council consultation requirements

**Deal dynamics:**
- Mandatory offers triggered at ownership thresholds
- Squeeze-out rules vary by country
- Employee consultation may delay or complicate
- Takeover resistance more common in some markets

### UK Targets

**Legal framework:**
Takeover Code governs public deals. Different from U.S. and continental Europe.

**Regulatory:**
- CMA competition review
- National Security and Investment Act for sensitive sectors
- FCA for financial services

**Deal dynamics:**
- Put up or shut up deadlines
- No break fees to bidders
- Offeror cannot walk away easily
- Employee and pension trustee opinions matter

### Asian Targets

**China:**
- MOFCOM and SAMR approval
- Foreign investment restrictions in many sectors
- SOE acquirers face additional scrutiny globally
- Local relationships critical

**Japan:**
- Negotiated deals preferred
- Hostile approaches rare and often unsuccessful
- Relationship-intensive process
- Cultural integration particularly challenging

**India:**
- Competition Commission of India review
- Foreign investment restrictions by sector
- Complex corporate structures
- Challenging regulatory environment

### Emerging Markets Generally

**Additional considerations:**
- Currency and repatriation risk
- Political and regulatory stability
- Local partner requirements in some sectors
- Corruption and compliance risk
- Weak rule of law in some jurisdictions
- Limited exit options

## Deal Structures

### Stock vs. Asset

**Stock deal:**
Acquire shares of target company. Simpler but inherits all liabilities.

**Asset deal:**
Acquire specific assets. Cleaner but tax and operational complications.

**Cross-border considerations:**
- Tax treatment varies by jurisdiction
- Transfer taxes and stamp duties
- Existing contracts and permits may not transfer
- Employment law implications

### Cash vs. Stock Consideration

**Cash:**
- Clear value for seller
- FX risk for buyer if foreign currency
- Financing required
- No ongoing equity relationship

**Stock:**
- Shares seller in upside/downside
- FX complexity if cross-listed
- May require registration
- Governance implications

**Mixed:**
Common in large cross-border deals. Balances interests and risks.

### Joint Ventures and Partnerships

**When used:**
- Market access with local expertise
- Regulatory requirements for local partner
- Risk sharing in uncertain markets
- Building toward full acquisition

**Challenges:**
- Governance complexity
- Exit mechanism disputes
- Different strategic objectives
- Cultural conflicts

## Common Failure Points

### Deal Stage Failures

**Regulatory blockage:**
Deals blocked by competition authorities or foreign investment review.

**Examples:** Broadcom/Qualcomm (CFIUS), Alstom/Siemens rail (EU), Nvidia/ARM (multiple jurisdictions)

**Valuation gap:**
Buyer and seller can't agree on value across different market contexts.

**Geopolitical intervention:**
Political pressure kills deals that might otherwise succeed.

### Integration Stage Failures

**Cultural clash:**
Management teams can't work together. Key talent leaves.

**Autonomy miscalculation:**
Too much integration destroys what made the target valuable. Too little prevents synergy capture.

**Synergy overestimation:**
Cross-border synergies are harder to achieve than forecasted.

**Execution failure:**
Integration plans not implemented effectively.

### The Statistics

Research consistently shows:
- 50-70% of cross-border deals fail to create expected value
- Cultural issues are the #1 cited reason for failure
- Integration planning is typically insufficient
- Synergy achievement averages 50-70% of projected

## Best Practices

### Pre-Deal

**Local expertise:**
Engage local advisors early. Understand the environment before committing.

**Cultural due diligence:**
Assess cultural fit explicitly. Not just numbers—management compatibility.

**Regulatory mapping:**
Understand approval requirements across all relevant jurisdictions before announcement.

**Conservative synergies:**
Apply significant haircuts to cross-border synergy estimates.

**Extended timeline:**
Budget more time than domestic deals. Delays are normal.

### Deal Execution

**Coordinated workstreams:**
Run multi-jurisdictional regulatory processes in coordination.

**Integration planning early:**
Start integration planning during due diligence, not after close.

**Clear governance:**
Establish clear decision-making processes across geographies.

**Communication:**
Over-communicate. Language and cultural barriers create misunderstanding.

### Integration

**Cultural integration focus:**
Make cultural integration an explicit priority with resources and attention.

**Leadership clarity:**
Clear reporting lines and accountability across regions.

**Quick wins:**
Identify and achieve quick wins to build momentum and credibility.

**Talent retention:**
Invest in retaining key talent from acquired company.

**Patient execution:**
Full integration takes longer than domestic. Be realistic about timeline.

## Interview Considerations

### What Interviewers Ask

**"Walk me through the key differences between a domestic and cross-border M&A transaction."**

Address: regulatory complexity (multiple jurisdictions), legal and structural differences (corporate law, employment, tax), currency considerations (valuation, financing, hedging), cultural factors (negotiation, integration), and timeline (typically 50%+ longer). Emphasize that the same analytical frameworks apply but with additional layers of complexity.

**"What are the biggest risks in cross-border M&A?"**

Regulatory risk (multi-jurisdictional approvals, CFIUS, foreign investment screening), cultural integration risk (top failure factor), currency risk (transaction and translation exposure), country/political risk (emerging markets especially), execution risk (longer timelines, more complexity), and synergy risk (harder to achieve cross-border synergies).

**"You're advising a U.S. company acquiring a German target. What key considerations would you raise?"**

Regulatory: EU Commission if thresholds met, German competition authority, German foreign investment screening. Employment: works council consultation requirements, co-determination for large companies. Legal: different corporate law, potential mandatory offer trigger. Tax: international structuring considerations. Cultural: German management style differences, consensus orientation. Timeline: longer than domestic U.S. deal.

### Technical Questions

**"How do you think about valuing a target in a different currency?"**

Options include: value in local currency and convert at spot/forward rate, value in USD using USD discount rate with currency-adjusted cash flows, or conduct parallel valuations. Key decisions: which comparable companies (local vs. global), what discount rate adjustment for country risk, how to project FX. In practice, typically value in functional currency and sensitize for FX.

**"What is CFIUS and when does it apply?"**

Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States reviews foreign acquisitions of U.S. businesses for national security concerns. Mandatory filing for certain transactions (TID categories, critical technology, critical infrastructure, sensitive personal data). Voluntary filing recommended for others with potential national security nexus. Can block transactions, require mitigation, or unwind completed deals. Increasing scrutiny, especially for China-linked investors. Timeline: 30-day review, potentially extended 45-day investigation, plus potential presidential review.

## Career Considerations

### Skills Development

Cross-border M&A experience develops valuable skills:

**Multi-disciplinary thinking:**
Integrating legal, tax, cultural, and business considerations.

**Communication:**
Working across languages and cultures.

**Coordination:**
Managing complex, multi-jurisdictional workstreams.

**Adaptability:**
Dealing with different business practices and expectations.

### Career Paths

**Investment banking:**
Cross-border expertise is valuable as companies globalize. Specialist coverage groups (European M&A, Asia M&A) or sector groups with cross-border activity.

**Corporate development:**
Companies with international M&A programs value experience.

**Private equity:**
Global funds need deal professionals who can execute across borders.

**Legal:**
Cross-border M&A lawyers are highly valued at top firms.

### The Reality

Cross-border transactions are:
- More interesting (complexity and variety)
- More demanding (longer hours, more travel, more stakeholders)
- More rewarding (larger deals, broader experience)
- More senior-demanding (junior bankers may have less visibility)

The experience is valuable—but be prepared for the intensity.

## The Bottom Line

Cross-border M&A represents a third of global deal activity. These transactions are strategic, complex, and frequently troubled.

**What makes them different:**
- Multi-jurisdictional regulatory requirements
- Different legal frameworks and business practices
- Cultural factors that affect execution and integration
- Currency and country risk considerations
- Longer timelines and higher costs

**What makes them succeed:**
- Local expertise and relationships
- Rigorous due diligence including cultural assessment
- Conservative synergy expectations
- Early and thorough integration planning
- Patient execution with clear governance

**The lesson:**
Cross-border M&A requires everything domestic deals require—plus significant additional capabilities. The complexity is real. So are the rewards for getting it right.

Companies will continue pursuing international acquisitions for strategic reasons. The professionals who can navigate the additional complexity will continue to be valuable.

Just don't underestimate how hard it is.
