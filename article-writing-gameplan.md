# Article Writing Gameplan

_A style guide for writing 150 finance articles that people actually want to read._

---

## The Core Philosophy

Most finance writing is terrible. It hides behind jargon. It buries the point. It mistakes complexity for sophistication.

We're doing the opposite.

Every article should feel like a smart friend explaining something over drinks. That friend happens to know a lot about finance. They respect your intelligence. They don't waste your time.

The goal is clarity that builds trust. Readers should finish an article feeling smarter, not confused. They should want to share it.

---

## Structure: The Invisible Skeleton

Good structure disappears. Readers don't notice it. They just feel carried along.

### The Opening (First 100 Words)

Start with a hook. Not a throat-clearing preamble. Not "In today's competitive landscape..."

Three hook types work well:

**The Provocative Claim**
> Goldman Sachs doesn't hire the smartest candidates. They hire the most prepared ones.

**The Concrete Scene**
> It's 2am on a Tuesday. You're in a windowless conference room. The MD just asked you to "refresh the comps" for the third time tonight.

**The Surprising Fact**
> Only 3% of investment banking analysts make Managing Director. The other 97% leave within seven years.

The hook earns the right to explain. It makes readers curious. It promises value.

### The Setup (Next 200 Words)

Tell readers what they'll learn. Be direct about it.

Bad: "This article will explore the various dimensions of..."
Good: "Here's what actually happens during PE recruiting—and how to navigate it."

Establish stakes. Why should they care? What's the cost of not knowing this?

### The Body (The Middle 80%)

Use sections with clear headers. Each section should deliver one main idea. Readers skim. Let them.

Within sections, follow this rhythm:
1. State the point
2. Explain why it's true
3. Give a concrete example
4. Address the obvious objection
5. Move on

Don't belabor. Make the point and advance.

### The Ending (Final 150 Words)

Resist the urge to summarize everything. Readers just read it. They remember.

Instead, do one of these:

**The Forward Look**: What should they do next?
**The Bigger Picture**: Zoom out. What does this mean for their career?
**The Honest Admission**: Acknowledge what the article couldn't cover.
**The Memorable Line**: Leave them with something quotable.

Bad endings trail off. Good endings land.

## Sentence Craft: The Hemingway Protocol

### Lead with Subject and Verb

Most sentences should start with who or what, then what they did.

Bad: "In order to successfully navigate the recruiting process, candidates should..."
Good: "Candidates should start networking six months early."

Bad: "It is often the case that interviewers will ask..."
Good: "Interviewers ask the same ten questions. Learn them."

The subject-verb opening creates momentum. It respects readers' time. It sounds confident.

### Keep Sentences Short (But Varied)

Aim for 10-15 words on average. But don't make every sentence the same length. That creates a monotonous drumbeat.

Mix it up:

> Banks recruit early. They recruit aggressively. The timeline has compressed dramatically over the past decade, with some firms extending offers to sophomores before they've taken a single finance class. This is insane. It's also reality.

See the rhythm? Short. Short. Long. Short. Short.

The long sentence earns its length. It carries information that needs unpacking. The short sentences punch.

### Choose Strong Verbs

Weak verbs hide behind adverbs. Strong verbs stand alone.

| Weak | Strong |
|------|--------|
| walked quickly | strode |
| said loudly | declared |
| looked carefully | examined |
| is indicative of | signals |
| provides assistance to | helps |

Finance writing loves weak verbs. "The analysis provides insight into..." Just say what the insight is.

### Cut Ruthlessly

First drafts are always too long. Cut these on sight:

- "In order to" → "To"
- "Due to the fact that" → "Because"
- "At this point in time" → "Now"
- "It is important to note that" → (delete entirely)
- "Basically" → (delete entirely)
- "Very" → (delete entirely, or find a stronger word)
- "In terms of" → (rewrite the sentence)

If a word doesn't earn its place, remove it.

## Voice and Tone

### Be Authoritative, Not Arrogant

State opinions directly. Don't hedge with "I think" or "In my opinion." The reader knows it's your opinion. You wrote it.

But stay humble about uncertainty. When you don't know something, say so. "The data here is limited" beats false confidence.

### Be Conversational, Not Casual

Write like you talk—but edit heavily. Spoken language is sloppy. Written language that sounds spoken is precise.

Contractions are fine. "Don't" and "won't" sound natural. "Cannot" sounds stiff.

Humor works when it's dry. Lucy Kellaway is funny because she's observant, not because she's trying to be funny. Notice the absurdity. Don't force jokes.

### Be Specific, Not Abstract

Abstract: "Networking is important for career success."
Specific: "Three coffee chats led to my Goldman interview."

Abstract: "The compensation in investment banking is substantial."
Specific: "First-year analysts at bulge brackets made $110K base plus $90K bonus in 2024."

Specifics persuade. Abstractions bore.

### Address the Reader Directly

Use "you" freely. It creates intimacy. It makes advice feel personal.

"One should prepare for interviews" → distant, academic.
"You should prepare for interviews" → direct, helpful.

## Article Types: Specific Guidance

### Technical Articles (T1)

The challenge: making complex concepts clear without dumbing them down.

**Structure that works:**
1. What is this thing? (One paragraph definition)
2. Why does it matter? (Stakes for interviews)
3. How does it work? (Step-by-step breakdown)
4. Common mistakes (What trips people up)
5. Practice questions (Real examples)

Use examples relentlessly. Abstract explanations of DCF don't help. Walking through an actual DCF does.

Format matters. Use tables for comparisons. Use numbered lists for processes. Use bullet points for lists of items. Don't make readers parse a wall of text.

### Firm Profiles (T2)

The challenge: being useful without being a press release.

**What readers actually want to know:**
- What's the culture really like? (Not the recruiting pitch)
- Who thrives here? (And who doesn't)
- What are the exit opportunities?
- How does recruiting work?
- What's the compensation?

Be honest about trade-offs. Every firm has them. Goldman has prestige but brutal hours. Evercore has deal flow but less training. Readers smell puff pieces. They trust balanced assessments.

Use quotes from people who've worked there. (Real ones, sourced properly.) Anonymous is fine. Fabricated is not.

### Career and Recruiting Articles (T3, T5)

The challenge: giving actionable advice, not generic platitudes.

**Bad advice:** "Network authentically and be yourself."
**Good advice:** "Send 10 cold emails per week. Use this template. Follow up after 5 days."

Specificity is kindness. Vague advice sounds wise but helps no one.

Acknowledge trade-offs. Every career decision involves giving something up. Don't pretend otherwise.

### DEI Articles (T6)

The challenge: being honest about real challenges without being preachy or performative.

Let people tell their own stories. Direct quotes from professionals who've navigated these challenges carry more weight than your analysis.

Avoid corporate DEI-speak. "Driving inclusive outcomes" means nothing. Concrete actions and specific experiences mean everything.

Acknowledge progress and problems. The industry has improved in some ways. It still has far to go in others. Both are true.

### Life in Finance Articles (T8)

The challenge: being real without being discouraging.

These articles need the most honesty. People are making life decisions. They deserve truth.

Don't romanticize the grind. Don't catastrophize it either. Describe it accurately. Let readers decide if it's for them.

Include coping strategies that actually work. Not "practice self-care." Instead: "Block 30 minutes for lunch. Protect it. Your body needs food."

## The Editing Process

### First Draft: Get It Down

Write fast. Don't edit while writing. That's two different brain modes. Mixing them slows both.

Your first draft will be too long and too clumsy. That's fine. That's what first drafts are.

### Second Draft: Structure Check

Read the whole thing. Does it flow? Does each section earn its place?

Cut any section that doesn't advance the argument. Move sections that appear in the wrong order. Add transitions where the logic jumps.

### Third Draft: Sentence Surgery

Now go line by line. Read sentences aloud. If you stumble, rewrite.

Apply the Hemingway protocol. Subject-verb. Strong verbs. Cut the fat.

### Fourth Draft: Fresh Eyes

Step away. Return the next day. Read it again.

You'll find things you missed. You always do.

## Common Pitfalls

### The Throat-Clear Opening

> "In today's fast-paced financial services industry, professionals face numerous challenges..."

Delete this. Start with your actual point.

### The Hedge Parade

> "It could potentially be argued that in some cases, this approach might sometimes be effective..."

Pick a position. State it. Defend it.

### The Jargon Avalanche

> "Leverage synergies to optimize value creation across the deal lifecycle."

If a sentence would embarrass you at a dinner party, rewrite it.

### The Endless Paragraph

Break up long paragraphs. Three to four sentences is plenty. White space is your friend.

### The Non-Ending

> "In conclusion, there are many factors to consider, and each person's situation is different."

This says nothing. End with something worth remembering.

---

## A Final Word

Writing well is thinking well. If the writing is unclear, the thinking probably is too.

Before you write, know your main point. Write it in one sentence. If you can't, you're not ready to write the article.

Every sentence should serve that point. Every paragraph should advance it. Every section should deepen it.

The reader's time is precious. Earn it.

---

## Quick Reference Checklist

Before publishing, verify:

- [ ] Opening hooks within first two sentences
- [ ] Main point clear by end of first section
- [ ] Each section has one core idea
- [ ] Sentences average 10-15 words
- [ ] Most sentences start with subject + verb
- [ ] Strong verbs, minimal adverbs
- [ ] Specific examples, not abstract claims
- [ ] Honest about trade-offs and limitations
- [ ] Ending lands (doesn't trail off)
- [ ] Read aloud without stumbling
